How bus interpolation works

How Next Stop moves buses between LTA updates

The Land Transport Authority tells the app two things about each bus: where its GPS last put it, and when it should reach your stop. New readings don't come often, so a bus that only moved when LTA spoke would jump around the map. Next Stop drives each bus smoothly along its route instead, paced so it reaches your stop when LTA says it will.

Four inputs

1. The route line
Each service's path is OneMap's bus route line. Where a stretch of that line is broken, the gap is filled with a line routed along OpenStreetMap streets (using OSRM) through LTA's list of stops. A service OneMap doesn't draw uses that street-routed line all the way. Buses can only move along this line.
2. Distance along the route
Every position becomes a number of metres from the start of the line. Each GPS reading is snapped onto the line, and your stop is a distance too, 5 m short of the pole so the bus pulls up just before it.
3. The arrival time
LTA's estimated arrival at your stop. It sets the pace.
4. Speed limits
50 km/h on ordinary roads, and 60 km/h on stretches where stops are 1.5 km or more apart, like expressways. In normal driving no bus goes faster than 60 km/h.

Finding the bus on the line

A route can pass the same road twice, in a loop or on the way back. So the app looks at every place on the line within 120 m of the GPS reading, and asks whether a bus there could reach your stop in the time LTA gives. A place is ruled out if the bus would need to go faster than 60 km/h, or move more than 2.5 times faster or 5 times slower than a bus normally would on that stretch, with a minute of slack either way. Among the places that fit, it prefers the one closest to where the bus was last time.

A speed map of every route

The app cuts each route into 5 m pieces and gives every piece a normal travel time. Pieces are slower where the bus is pulling into or out of a stop, on bends, and where stops are close together. They are never faster than the speed limit. Adding up the pieces gives the normal time between any two points on the route.

Pacing to the arrival time

From the speed map, the app knows the normal time from the bus to your stop. LTA says how many seconds are actually left. Dividing one by the other gives the pace:

pace = normal time left ÷ seconds LTA says are left

Pace is kept between 0.2 and 2.5. An invisible shadow bus drives at that pace through the speed map, never above the limit, and brakes smoothly into the stop. The bus you see follows the shadow, closing the gap over about two seconds so it never jerks. If LTA gives no arrival time, the bus uses a pace learned from how fast it has really been moving between readings.

When a new reading arrives

The shadow moves to the new spot on the line straight away, and the bus you see drives to catch up. If the new reading is well ahead, up to 1.5 km, the bus may briefly go up to 90 km/h (1.5 times the 60 km/h cap) to get there, then settles back. If the new reading is a little behind, the bus holds still until the shadow catches up, instead of reversing. A reading more than 600 m behind moves the bus straight there.

Never ahead of what we know

While a bus is more than 80 m from your stop, it only drives up to 80 m short of it, until a newer GPS reading shows the bus closer. So a bus on the map doesn't reach your stop before the real one does.
